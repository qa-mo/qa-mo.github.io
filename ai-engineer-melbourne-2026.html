Main Themes from Day 1

1. Production Reality, Not Prototypes

Companies shared real production experiences - what worked, what failed, and actual costs. Examples: $1.5M/year productivity gains at SEEK, systems handling 250M+ users at Canva, and 10% engagement lifts at REA Group.

2. Security Through Smart Design

You can't just "block bad things" with AI systems. Instead, design systems that stay safe even when things go wrong. Think seatbelts, not just careful driving.

3. Memory is Critical

The difference between a demo and a production AI agent is whether it can remember what it's doing across hours or days - like the difference between a sticky note and a proper filing system.

4. Testing is Everything

Having good ways to measure if your AI is working correctly is more important than tweaking prompts. It's like having a thermometer vs. just guessing if food is cooked.

5. Cost Matters

AI costs directly affect what features you can build. It's like choosing between express shipping or regular - sometimes the cheaper option is fine.

1. Building Long-Running AI Agents ✓ Full Transcript

11:00-11:20 AM  |  Speakers: Pham Chi & Vamsi Ramakrishnan (Google Cloud)
What it's about: Building AI systems that can run for days or weeks, not just minutes - like a long-running project assistant instead of a quick calculator.

Key Takeaways in Plain English:

Stop Treating AI as the Main Thing

Instead of building everything around a big AI model, treat AI like a tool in your toolbox. It's like using a calculator to help you do accounting - the calculator isn't the accountant, it's just one tool.

Technical note: "We only use large language models as a dependency, not as a first class citizen."

Smaller Models Can Work Better

You don't always need the biggest, most expensive AI model. They're training smaller, specialized models that work faster and cheaper. Think: using a pocket calculator instead of a supercomputer for simple math.

Result: Training went from taking a week to just 3-5 hours.

Memory is the Hard Part

When AI agents run for days, they need to remember what they did yesterday without forgetting or getting confused. This is still an unsolved problem - like trying to keep a consistent diary that multiple people write in.

Real Example: Voice AI at Scale

They built a system that handles a million phone calls per day using Rust (a programming language known for speed and reliability). When you're handling that many calls, even tiny delays matter.

The Philosophy: Own Your AI

"We believe that you should own your AI. The whole stack should be in your hands." - Meaning: don't be completely dependent on someone else's AI service that could change prices or features anytime.

Real Project: They had an AI agent work on converting Linux kernel code to Rust for over 10 months, gradually improving from 12% success rate to much higher.

2. Securing AI Systems ✓ Full Transcript

2:10-2:28 PM  |  Speaker: Adesh Gairola (CTO, raxIT Labs)
What it's about: How to keep AI systems safe when attackers can trick them with clever messages (called "prompt injection").

Key Takeaways in Plain English:

You Can't Just Block Bad Inputs

"We can't filter our way out of prompt injection." Traditional security (like spam filters) doesn't work well with AI because attackers are too creative. It's like trying to childproof a house by listing every dangerous thing - you'll always miss something.

Design for When Things Go Wrong

Instead of hoping your AI won't be tricked, design it so that even if it IS tricked, it can't do anything harmful. Like how a car has airbags AND seatbelts - defense in depth.

The Three S's Framework

1. Scope (Limit What AI Can Do): Use two AI systems - one that handles dangerous tasks and one that processes untrusted user input. They can't both be compromised at once. Like having a security guard who checks people before they enter the main building.

2. Sign (Track Everything): Keep a record of every action the AI takes and who authorized it. Like a security camera log - if something goes wrong, you can trace it back.

3. Stop (Add Gates): Before the AI does anything important, run it through a policy checker that says "is this action allowed?" Like needing manager approval for big purchases.

"Least Agency" Principle

Give your AI the minimum amount of power it needs to do its job. Don't give a customer service AI access to your entire database when it only needs to look up orders.

Track Sensitive Information

If personal information (like names, emails, addresses) goes into the AI system, track where it goes and make sure it doesn't leak out. Like putting "confidential" stickers on sensitive documents.

Bottom Line

Shift from hope-based security (hoping nothing goes wrong) to proof-based security (proving the system stays safe even when things go wrong).

3. Optimizing AI Performance ✓ Full Transcript

2:10-2:28 PM  |  Speaker: Ron Au (Canva/Leonardo.Ai)
What it's about: Using "multi-armed bandits" (a smart testing strategy) to find the best AI settings faster and cheaper than traditional A/B testing.

Key Takeaways in Plain English:

What's a Multi-Armed Bandit?

Imagine you're at a casino with multiple slot machines. You want to find which one pays out best, but you also want to win money while testing. Multi-armed bandits help you balance "trying new machines" (exploration) vs "using the best machine you've found so far" (exploitation).

For AI, this means automatically finding the best settings while still serving real users good results.

Real Example: Testing AI Personality

They tested four different conversation styles for their AI:

  • Formal (professional and serious)
  • Warm (friendly and approachable) - This won!
  • Direct (straight to the point)
  • Playful (casual and fun) - This did worst

The system automatically sent more users to "warm" as it learned it worked best, without waiting for a fixed test period to end.

Save Money on Testing

Instead of running thousands of tests with fixed numbers, use confidence intervals. Stop testing early when you're confident you've found the answer. Like stopping a taste test when 90% of people clearly prefer option A - you don't need to test 1000 more people.

Two Main Algorithms

Epsilon Greedy: Simple approach - use the best option 90% of the time, try something random 10% of the time. Easy to understand but not the most efficient.

Thompson Sampling: Smarter approach using probability. Automatically balances exploration and exploitation based on how confident you are about each option.

Why This Matters

Traditional A/B testing locks you into testing for a fixed time, even if one option is clearly winning. Multi-armed bandits adapt in real-time, finding the best option faster and serving better results to users during testing.

4. AI Governance & Ethics Panel ✓ Full Transcript

2:00-2:25 PM  |  Moderator: Andrew Murphy | Panelists: Aubrey Blanche, Hamish Songsmith, Nick Lothian
What it's about: How organizations mess up when adding AI, and how to do it right - covering governance, ethics, and common mistakes.

Key Takeaways in Plain English:

Top 3 Mistakes Companies Make

1. Naive Risk Assessment
Mistake: "We got security approval for one AI chatbot, so all our AI projects are secure now!"
Reality: Each AI use case is different. A customer service chatbot has different risks than an AI that writes code or processes financial data. You need separate assessments.

2. Wrong Success Metrics
Mistake: "Our AI makes employees 20% faster!"
Reality: If you focus only on speed, you might get low-quality work done faster. Better to ask: "Does this actually help our customers?" or "Does this help us accomplish our mission?"

3. "Vibe-Based" Rollouts
Mistake: "Everyone else is using ChatGPT for customer service, let's do it too!"
Reality: Just because something is trending doesn't mean it fits your organization. You need a strategy, not just copying what's popular.

Privacy & Model Access

There's debate about AI model access:

  • Chinese companies often release "open" AI models anyone can use
  • American companies (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google) keep their best models restricted
  • Question: Is this about security concerns or protecting business interests?

For privacy: Tools exist for removing personal info from data (PII redaction), and techniques like "federated learning" let you train AI without centralizing sensitive data.

HR & Governance Reality

Some people think: "Just fire the HR department and let AI handle it!"
Panel's view: Bad idea. AI doesn't replace the need for proper people management and governance. You still need humans to handle the human side of work.

How to Do It Right

  • Frame AI adoption around customer value or mission impact, not just "faster and cheaper"
  • Encourage smart experimentation, but with proper oversight
  • Build governance that scales - don't assume one approval covers everything
  • Consider both the technical side and the human side

Key Quote

"The most common mistake companies make when rolling out AI is assuming that because they've done the compliance framework for one implementation, it extends to all other uses."

5. Flue: The Agent Harness Framework ✓ Full Transcript

5:10-5:28 PM  |  Speaker: Michael Hart (Senior Principal Engineer, Cloudflare)
What it's about: Flue is a headless TypeScript framework for building AI agents that can be programmed and deployed anywhere - think of it as a way to create custom AI workers that run automatically.

Key Takeaways in Plain English:

What is Flue?

Flue is a framework for building AI agents that run without a user interface ("headless"). Unlike interactive tools like Claude Code that you click around in, Flue lets you write code that controls AI agents automatically.

Think of it like: writing a robot script that does tasks for you, instead of manually clicking buttons yourself.

Two Types of AI Workers

1. Workflows (Run and Return): Do one job and finish. Like sending an errand runner to the store - they go, buy what you need, come back, and they're done.

2. Agents (Long-Running with Identity): Stay active and keep working over time. Like hiring an assistant who checks your email every day and handles things continuously.

Works Everywhere

Flue can run on any platform: Node, Deno, Vercel, Cloudflare Workers, and more. It's "platform-agnostic" - meaning you write it once and deploy it anywhere.

Like writing a recipe that works in any kitchen, not just one specific brand of oven.

Real-World Example: Astro CMS

Astro (a popular website builder) uses Flue for their GitHub issue triage. When someone reports a bug:

  • Flue automatically investigates the issue
  • Does root cause analysis (figures out what's wrong)
  • No human needs to manually sort through every bug report

Cloudflare's Internal Use: "Cloudflare OS"

Cloudflare built an internal platform where both technical and non-technical employees can:

  • Access a library of pre-built "skills" (reusable AI capabilities)
  • Create isolated workspaces for different teams
  • Run multiple AI agents for different purposes

It's like having an app store of AI helpers inside the company.

Status & Availability

Flue is pre-1.0 (still being refined) but approaching its official release. It's completely written in TypeScript and fully programmable.

Who it's for: Developers who want to automate AI tasks without relying on interactive tools.

6. How Canva Built Agentic Support at Scale ✓ Full Transcript

5:10-5:28 PM  |  Speakers: Sergey Iakovlev & Sahil Bahl (Senior ML Engineers, Canva)
What it's about: How Canva built and scaled AI-powered customer support to serve 250+ million users, focusing on observability (tracking what AI is doing) and evaluation (measuring if it's working well).

Key Takeaways in Plain English:

The Challenge: Supporting 250M+ Users with AI

Canva serves over 250 million users. That's a LOT of customer support requests. They needed AI to help, but had to make sure it worked reliably at massive scale.

Like running a restaurant that serves millions of meals a day - you need systems to ensure quality stays consistent.

Observability: Tracking What AI Does

The Problem: "You pay for all the traces that you capture" - tracking every single AI interaction is expensive.

Solution: Trace Sampling: Instead of recording everything, they sample (record a portion) and use async jobs to enrich the data later.

Think of it like: instead of video recording every customer interaction, you take snapshots and fill in details later when needed.

Shadow Run Testing (Zero User Impact)

When testing a new version of their AI support system:

  • Run the new version alongside live traffic
  • Users only see the old, proven version
  • Compare results between old and new in the background
  • If new version works better, gradually roll it out

Like test-driving a new recipe in the back kitchen while still serving the regular menu to customers.

LLM-as-Judge: Having AI Grade AI

Instead of humans manually reviewing thousands of AI responses, they use AI to evaluate AI.

Key approach: Multiple narrow judges - Instead of one AI judging everything, have specialized AIs check different aspects:

  • One AI checks if the tone is friendly
  • Another checks if information is accurate
  • Another checks if the response is helpful

Like having specialized food critics instead of one person judging everything.

Multi-Layered Evaluation

They don't rely on just one way to measure quality. They use:

  • Automated tests (AI checking AI)
  • Human review (spot-checking important cases)
  • Live metrics (tracking real user satisfaction)
  • A/B testing (comparing different versions)

Key Principles

  • Traces as primary artifacts: Recording what happened is crucial for debugging and improvement
  • Multi-layered evaluation: Don't rely on just one measurement method
  • Democratize quality assurance: Everyone on the team should be able to check quality, not just specialists

7. Leadership Panel: AI & Team Success ✓ Full Transcript

End of Day 1  |  Leadership Track Panel Discussion
What it's about: A frank discussion about how AI is affecting software teams, the limits of AI, and what leaders need to focus on to ensure their teams succeed.

Key Takeaways in Plain English:

The Big Reality Check

"You can't replace five seniors with five juniors with AI. That's what everyone's trying to do."

This was the most direct message of the panel. Companies think: "We'll hire cheaper junior developers and AI will make up the difference." The panelists say this doesn't work.

Why? Experience matters. Senior developers know:

  • Which problems to solve (and which to ignore)
  • How to architect systems that scale
  • What shortcuts will cause problems later
  • How to mentor and guide others

AI can help write code faster, but it can't replace wisdom and judgment.

Smaller Teams, Better Support

Modern teams can be smaller but deliver more - IF they have the right support structures:

  • Good tools and infrastructure
  • Clear processes
  • Ability to focus without constant interruptions
  • AI to handle repetitive tasks

It's not about cramming more work onto fewer people. It's about removing obstacles so talented people can do their best work.

Breaking Down Silos

Traditional boundaries (frontend vs backend, dev vs ops) are becoming less relevant. With AI assistance, engineers can work across more areas.

But this doesn't mean one person does everything - it means teams collaborate more fluidly.

Human-in-the-Loop Guardrails

When using AI for development, you need safety checks:

  • Data-driven rules based on past bugs and accessibility issues
  • Automated checks before code goes live
  • Human review for critical changes

Think of it like spell-check in a word processor - it catches obvious mistakes, but you still need to think about what you're writing.

Leadership Accountability

The panel emphasized: if your team isn't thriving, that's a leadership problem, not a people problem.

Leaders must ensure:

  • People have what they need to succeed
  • The environment supports growth and learning
  • Expectations are clear and reasonable
  • Team members feel valued and supported

Key message: Don't blame individuals or reduce headcount when the real issue is lack of support or poor systems.

Bottom Line

AI is a tool that can amplify what good engineers do. It won't fix bad processes, replace experience, or make up for poor leadership. Focus on building strong teams with the right support, and use AI to help them do more of what they're already good at.
